## Part 2: Interview questions

### 1. "Our fraud model is 99% accurate, so a flag means 99% likely fraud." What is wrong?

**Strong answer:** "99% accurate" describes how the model treats each group, not what a flag means. Say
fraud is 1 in 1,000 transactions and the model catches 99% of fraud and wrongly flags 1% of honest
ones. Out of a million transactions, about 990 frauds are flagged, but so are 1% of the 999,000 honest
ones, about 10,000. So only about 990 of roughly 11,000 flags are fraud: 9%. The rarer the fraud, the
more the honest majority's small error rate dominates. What we should quote is precision at the real
fraud rate, and decide what to do with a flag (block, review, step-up verification) from that number
and the cost of each mistake.

**Weak answer:** "Accuracy is misleading on imbalanced data, so we should use F1 instead."

**Why the weak one fails:** it names a metric but never explains why a flag means 9%, which was the
question; a PM cannot act on "use F1". It also skips the base rate, the actual cause.

**Follow-up an interviewer would ask:** "Fraud doubles during the holidays. What happens to precision,
recall and the false positive rate?" (Precision rises; recall and false positive rate stay the same if
the model's behaviour per group does not change.)

### 2. Why train classifiers with log-loss rather than MSE on probabilities?

**Strong answer:** Log-loss is the negative log-likelihood of a Bernoulli (or categorical) model of the
labels, so minimizing it is maximum likelihood: the principled fit for a model that outputs
probabilities. It also punishes confident mistakes heavily (predicting 0.01 for a true positive costs
$-\log 0.01 = 4.6$), while MSE caps the penalty at 1, so MSE barely distinguishes "wrong" from "confidently
wrong". With a sigmoid output, the log-loss gradient with respect to the logit is simply $p - y$, which
stays large when the model is badly wrong; MSE's gradient gets multiplied by $\sigma'(z)$, which vanishes when
the sigmoid saturates, so learning stalls exactly when the model is most wrong. For logistic regression
log-loss is also convex in the weights; MSE through a sigmoid is not.

**Weak answer:** "Log-loss is the standard for classification and works better in practice."

**Why the weak one fails:** it gives no reason; an interviewer is checking whether you know the
likelihood view and the gradient behaviour.

**Follow-up an interviewer would ask:** "What does log-loss become for $K$ classes, and what is its
minimum value on a dataset where the labels are themselves noisy?" (Categorical cross-entropy; the
minimum is the entropy of the labels, not zero, A6.4.)

### 3. L2 regularization from a Bayesian point of view.

**Strong answer:** Training with squared error is maximum likelihood under Gaussian noise. If, before
seeing data, we believe each weight is drawn from a Gaussian centred at zero with variance $\tau^2$, Bayes'
theorem says the posterior is likelihood times prior, and maximizing it (MAP) means minimizing the
squared error plus $\frac{\sigma^2}{\tau^2}\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert^2$. So $\lambda = \sigma^2/\tau^2$: strong regularization means we believe the weights are small
(tight prior) or the data is noisy. With more data the likelihood term grows and the prior matters
less. The view also explains practical rules: do not penalize the intercept (we have no belief that it is
near zero) and standardize features so one prior width makes sense for every weight. A Laplace prior
gives L1 instead, which pushes some weights exactly to zero.

**Weak answer:** "L2 adds the squared weights to the loss to prevent overfitting by keeping weights small."

**Why the weak one fails:** correct but not what was asked; it has no prior, no posterior and no
interpretation of $\lambda$.

**Follow-up an interviewer would ask:** "In scikit-learn's `LogisticRegression`, what does `C` correspond
to?" (The prior variance $\tau^2$: larger $C$ means weaker regularization.)

### 4. What is the CLT, and why does it matter for model accuracy or A/B tests?

**Strong answer:** The CLT says the average of many independent draws with finite variance is
approximately Gaussian, with mean equal to the true mean and standard deviation $\sigma/\sqrt{n}$, whatever the
distribution of a single draw. Test accuracy is an average of 0/1 correctness indicators, and a
conversion rate is an average of 0/1 conversions, so both have an approximately Gaussian sampling
distribution with standard error $\sqrt{p(1 - p)/n}$. That gives error bars: 90% accuracy on 1,000 examples is
$\pm 1.9$ points at 95%, so a 0.5-point improvement is noise. In an A/B test the difference of two means is
also approximately Gaussian, which is what the usual z-test uses, and it lets you compute the sample
size needed before starting. Caveats: the examples must be independent (several rows per user break
this), and small samples of very skewed metrics like revenue need much larger $n$ or the bootstrap.

**Weak answer:** "The CLT says data becomes normal when you have enough of it, so we can use normal-based
tests."

**Why the weak one fails:** it confuses the data with the sampling distribution of the mean, which is the
most common misunderstanding of the CLT.

**Follow-up an interviewer would ask:** "Your A/B test randomizes by user but you compute the conversion
rate per page view. What goes wrong?" (Page views from one user are correlated, so the real standard
error is larger than $\sqrt{p(1 - p)/n_{\text{views}}}$; analyse per user.)

### 5. Correlation versus independence, with a production example.

**Strong answer:** Independence means knowing one variable tells you nothing at all about the other;
correlation measures only how well a straight line relates them. Independent implies zero correlation,
but not the reverse: a U-shaped relation (latency high at both very low load, from cold caches, and very
high load, from saturation) can have correlation near zero while being strongly dependent. In
production this matters for alerting and feature selection: dropping a metric or a feature because its
correlation with the target is near zero can throw away a strong non-linear or rare-event signal. It
also matters for sums: the variance of a total is only the sum of variances if the parts are
uncorrelated; correlated services that peak together need more capacity than the sum of their
variances suggests. So plot the relationship, or use a measure that catches non-linear dependence
(mutual information, A6.4, or a tree model's importance).

**Weak answer:** "Correlation means two things move together, and independence means they don't."

**Why the weak one fails:** it treats the two as opposites of each other and misses that zero
correlation does not imply independence, which is the point of the question.

**Follow-up an interviewer would ask:** "Two features have correlation 0.95. Is that a problem for a
linear model? For a tree?" (Unstable, hard-to-interpret coefficients for the linear model, A4.4's
ill-conditioning; little effect on a tree's predictions, but split importance gets shared.)

### 6. Modelling errors per minute on a service.

**Strong answer:** Counts of events in a fixed interval suggest a Poisson model with rate $\lambda$ per minute,
which assumes events happen independently at a constant rate. To check it: compare the mean and
variance of per-minute counts (Poisson has variance equal to the mean); compare the observed frequency
of 0, 1, 2, … errors with the Poisson pmf at the fitted rate; and look at the time series for bursts and
daily patterns. Errors usually fail both assumptions: they come in bursts during incidents and deploys
(variance far above the mean, overdispersion) and the rate varies with traffic. Fixes: model the rate as
a function of traffic and time (errors per request, or a Poisson regression with traffic as an input), or
use a negative binomial distribution, which allows extra variance. For alerting, the error *ratio* to
requests is usually more stable than the raw count.

**Weak answer:** "A normal distribution with the mean and standard deviation of the counts, and alert at
three standard deviations."

**Why the weak one fails:** counts are discrete, non-negative and skewed at low rates; a Gaussian
threshold gives wrong false-alarm rates, and the answer never checks any assumption.

**Follow-up an interviewer would ask:** "Under a Poisson model with rate 2 per minute, how surprising is
a minute with 8 errors?" ($P(X \ge 8) \approx 0.001$: rare, but at 1,440 minutes a day it happens about once
or twice daily by chance alone.)

### 7. Estimating a quantity with no closed form (expected cost of a retry policy).

**Strong answer:** Monte Carlo: write a simulator of the process (each attempt fails with some
probability, possibly correlated; each retry adds latency and cost; the policy decides backoff and
cap), run it many times with independent random draws, and average the cost. By the law of large
numbers the average converges to the expectation, and by the CLT its error bar is $\pm 1.96\,s/\sqrt{n}$, where $s$ is
the standard deviation of the simulated costs. To choose $n$: run a pilot, estimate $s$, and solve
$n = (1.96\,s/E)^2$ for the precision $E$ you need; to compare two policies, simulate both with common random
numbers so the noise cancels in the difference. Validate the simulator on a case with a known answer
(no retries, or independent failures where the expected number of attempts is $1/(1 - p_{\text{fail}})$), and
report the result with its error bar.

**Weak answer:** "Run the simulation a few thousand times and take the average."

**Why the weak one fails:** no error bar, no way to pick $n$, and no validation; "a few thousand" may be far
too few for a heavy-tailed cost or far too many for a smooth one.

**Follow-up an interviewer would ask:** "The cost distribution is heavy-tailed (rare cascading failures).
What changes?" (Many more samples, a bootstrap interval or percentiles instead of only the mean, and
possibly importance sampling to see the rare events often enough.)

### 8. What is MLE? The Gaussian mean, and when MLE is a poor choice.

**Strong answer:** Maximum likelihood picks the parameters that make the observed data most probable:
$\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}} = \arg\max\prod_i p(x_i \mid \boldsymbol{\theta})$, in practice minimizing the negative log-likelihood, a sum over examples. For a
Gaussian with known $\sigma$ the NLL is $\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum(x_i - \mu)^2$ plus a constant; its derivative $-\frac{1}{\sigma^2}\sum(x_i - \mu)$
is zero when $\mu = \bar{x}$, so the MLE of the mean is the sample mean, and the same calculation shows that
least squares is MLE under Gaussian noise. MLE can be poor with little data relative to the number of
parameters (it overfits; a prior, i.e. MAP or regularization, helps), when the MLE is at a boundary or
does not exist (a coin with 3 heads in 3 flips gives $\hat{p} = 1$; separable data in logistic regression sends
the weights to infinity), when the assumed model is wrong (Gaussian MLE is ruined by outliers that a
heavy-tailed model would absorb), and it can be biased (the variance MLE divides by $n$).

**Weak answer:** "MLE finds the parameters that maximize the probability of the parameters given the
data."

**Why the weak one fails:** that is the posterior, i.e. MAP. Confusing $p(\text{data} \mid \boldsymbol{\theta})$ with $p(\boldsymbol{\theta} \mid \text{data})$ is
the error the question is designed to catch.

**Follow-up an interviewer would ask:** "Derive the MLE of the variance. Is it unbiased?"
($\hat{\sigma}^2 = \frac1n\sum(x_i - \bar{x})^2$; no, its expectation is $\frac{n - 1}{n}\sigma^2$.)
