4. **A responsibility $\gamma_{ik}$ is the posterior probability that component $k$ produced point $i$. The E-step computes it with Bayes' rule:

## Part 2: Interview questions

### 1. Explain k-means. How do you choose $k$, and how do you convince yourself that the clusters are real?

**Strong answer:** k-means minimizes inertia, the sum of squared distances from each point to its cluster's centroid, by alternating two steps: assign each
point to the nearest centroid, then move each centroid to the mean of its points. Each step lowers the inertia, so it converges, but to a local minimum, so I
use k-means++ seeding and several restarts (`n_init=10`) and standardize features first, because it is all Euclidean distances. To choose $k$ I scan a range
and look at the elbow of the inertia curve and the silhouette score, knowing that neither is decisive: inertia always falls, and the silhouette favours round,
well-separated blobs. To check that clusters are real, I refit with different seeds and on bootstrap resamples and compare the assignments with the adjusted
Rand index; unstable clusters are not findings. Finally I profile each cluster in original units and ask whether the people who will use it can name it and
act on it differently.
**Weak answer:** "k-means groups points into k clusters. You pick k with the elbow method, and if the silhouette is high the clusters are good."
**Why the weak one fails:** no algorithm, no mention of local minima, scaling or restarts, and it treats the elbow and silhouette as proof rather than
evidence; nothing on stability.
**Follow-up an interviewer would ask:** "Your silhouette is highest at $k = 2$ but marketing wants five segments. What do you do?"

### 2. When would you not use k-means? What would you use instead?

**Strong answer:** k-means assumes roughly round clusters of similar size and spread, that every point belongs to a cluster, and that I know $k$. I would
not use it for curved or nested shapes (rings, two moons), for clusters of very different size or density, for data with real noise that should stay
unassigned, or when I only have a distance matrix rather than coordinates. For arbitrary shapes and noise in low dimensions, DBSCAN or HDBSCAN: they find
dense regions, choose the number of clusters, and label sparse points as noise; but DBSCAN's $\varepsilon$ is in feature units, one $\varepsilon$ fails when
densities differ, and in high dimensions distances concentrate. For tilted or unequal elliptical clusters with soft membership, a Gaussian mixture. For a
tree of nested groupings or a custom distance, hierarchical clustering, which costs $O(n^2)$ memory and, with single linkage, chains across thin bridges.
**Weak answer:** "When the data is big, use DBSCAN, it's better because you don't need k."
**Why the weak one fails:** k-means is the one that scales best; the answer names no assumption k-means violates and no weakness of the alternative.
**Follow-up an interviewer would ask:** "How would you choose DBSCAN's $\varepsilon$?"

### 3. What is the difference between a Gaussian mixture model and k-means, and how is a GMM fitted?

**Strong answer:** A GMM models the data as a weighted sum of Gaussians, each with its own mean, covariance and weight, so clusters can be tilted,
elongated and of different sizes, and every point gets a probability of belonging to each component instead of a hard label. It is also a density model, so
$-\log p(\mathbf{x})$ is an anomaly score. It is fitted by EM: the E-step computes responsibilities with Bayes' rule under the current parameters, the M-step
re-estimates weights, means and covariances as responsibility-weighted averages, and the log-likelihood never decreases. k-means is the limiting case with
equal weights and equal spherical covariances shrinking to zero. Like k-means it finds local maxima, so I use several starts (k-means initialization), keep
`reg_covar` so components cannot collapse onto a point, standardize, and choose $K$ and the covariance type by BIC.
**Weak answer:** "A GMM is a soft k-means. You fit it with gradient descent on the likelihood."
**Why the weak one fails:** "soft k-means" misses the per-component covariances and the density, and it names the wrong fitting method; nothing on local
maxima, degeneracy or choosing $K$.
**Follow-up an interviewer would ask:** "Why can the likelihood of a GMM go to infinity, and what stops it?"

### 4. Explain PCA. How do you choose the number of components, and what are the common mistakes in a pipeline?

**Strong answer:** PCA centres the data and finds orthogonal directions of maximal variance: the eigenvectors of the covariance matrix, computed in practice
as the right singular vectors of the centred data, with variance $\sigma_j^2/(n - 1)$ along each. Projecting onto the top $k$ keeps the most variance and,
equivalently, gives the smallest squared reconstruction error, which equals the dropped variance. I choose $k$ by the downstream task: cross-validate the
model with PCA inside the pipeline over several $k$, using the cumulative explained-variance curve (say 90–95%) only as a starting point, because high
variance is not the same as useful for the label. Common mistakes: not standardizing features in different units (one large-unit feature becomes PC1),
fitting PCA on all rows before splitting, using PCA before tree models that do not need it, reading meaning into component signs or names that flip on refit,
and expecting it to capture non-linear structure.
**Weak answer:** "PCA reduces dimensions by keeping the most important features. Keep components until you have 95% of the variance."
**Why the weak one fails:** components are not features but mixtures of all of them; the 95% rule is stated as the answer; no mention of centring, scaling
or leakage.
**Follow-up an interviewer would ask:** "Why compute PCA with the SVD rather than by eigendecomposing the covariance matrix?"

### 5. A colleague shows a UMAP plot with seven islands and proposes seven campaigns. What do you say?

**Strong answer:** The plot is a good reason to look closer, not evidence of seven segments. UMAP and t-SNE preserve local neighbourhoods, but island sizes
and the gaps between islands are largely artefacts, the number of islands changes with `n_neighbors` (or perplexity) and the seed, and both can draw
convincing clusters in data with none. So I would cluster in the original standardized feature space (or on a PCA reduction), check the number of
clusters with silhouette and stability across seeds and bootstrap resamples, rerun the UMAP at several settings, and profile each candidate segment in
original units: if two islands have the same spend, frequency and tenure, they are one campaign. Then I would ask what each campaign would do differently,
and test it with a holdout (A/B test) before rolling out seven.
**Weak answer:** "UMAP is better than t-SNE at preserving global structure, so the seven islands are real; go ahead."
**Why the weak one fails:** it treats a visualization as a clustering, ignores sensitivity to hyperparameters, and proposes spending money without any
stability or business check.
**Follow-up an interviewer would ask:** "How would you show your colleague that the plot can mislead?"

### 6. Design an anomaly detector for card transactions with no fraud labels.

**Strong answer:** First the operational facts: how many alerts a day the analysts can review, and what a missed fraud versus a wasted review costs. Features
per transaction and per card or merchant context (amount relative to the card's history, time since the last transaction, new country or merchant), scaled
robustly. Then two complementary detectors: an isolation forest (no scaling needed, many features, fast) and a density score such as k-NN distance or LOF,
plus a few explainable rules for known patterns. The threshold comes from the review budget: flag the top $B$ per day, not from `contamination` or the
0.5 rule of thumb. Evaluation: get a few labels by having analysts review a sample of top-ranked and random cases, then report precision at the budget and
average precision with bootstrap intervals, because the labels will be few. After launch I log every analyst verdict, track precision@budget over time, and
move to a supervised model (or use anomaly scores as its features) once enough confirmed cases accumulate.
**Weak answer:** "Train an isolation forest with contamination 0.01 and block everything it flags."
**Why the weak one fails:** no review budget, no evaluation, `contamination` used as if it were knowledge of the fraud rate, and an automatic block on an
"unusual" signal that will mostly flag legitimate customers.
**Follow-up an interviewer would ask:** "Most of your top alerts turn out to be big legitimate customers. What do you change?"

### 7. PCA, t-SNE and UMAP: when would you use each, and which can be features?

**Strong answer:** PCA is linear, deterministic up to signs, fast, has a `transform` for new rows, and its axes have a meaning (variance explained, loadings),
so it is the first look at data, a compression step before a model, and a pre-step (to about 50 components) for t-SNE or UMAP. t-SNE is for 2-D pictures of
local neighbourhood structure: it has no `transform`, its global layout, cluster sizes and distances are unreliable, and it is slow on large data. UMAP is
similar in purpose, faster, and has a `transform`, but its layout also depends strongly on `n_neighbors`, `min_dist` and the seed. As model features, PCA
components are standard (fitted inside the pipeline on training rows only). I would not feed t-SNE coordinates to a model; UMAP coordinates can be used with
care, fitted on training rows only, but they are unstable across refits, so the model must be retrained with them.
**Weak answer:** "t-SNE and UMAP are better than PCA because they are non-linear, so use them for everything, including features."
**Why the weak one fails:** non-linear does not mean better; it ignores the missing `transform`, instability and leakage, and the strengths of PCA.
**Follow-up an interviewer would ask:** "Why run PCA before t-SNE?"

### 8. How do you know an unsupervised segmentation and anomaly detector are working in production?

**Strong answer:** Without labels you check usefulness and stability instead of accuracy. For the segmentation: stability (ARI between this month's and
last month's assignments on the same customers, and against bootstrap refits), segment sizes and profiles over time, and the business test: do the actions
tied to segments move the target metric in a controlled experiment? I keep segment identities stable across refits (match new centroids to old ones, or
assign with fixed centroids and refit on a schedule), because dashboards and CRM rules depend on them. For the anomaly detector: the alert volume, the share of
alerts reviewers confirm (precision at the budget), the time to review, and drift in the score distribution and inputs (J2.2); reviewer verdicts are logged
and become the labels that let me evaluate, retune or replace it with a supervised model.
**Weak answer:** "Check that the silhouette score stays high and that the anomaly detector keeps flagging about 1% of rows."
**Why the weak one fails:** both are internal numbers that can stay constant while the system becomes useless; no link to actions, reviewers or stability.
**Follow-up an interviewer would ask:** "Marketing reports that segment 3 now behaves like segment 1 used to. What happened, and how do you prevent it?"
