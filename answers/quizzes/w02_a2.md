## Part 2: Interview questions

### 1. What does the weight matrix of a dense layer do to its input, geometrically? Why does a network need non-linear activations?

**Strong answer:** A weight matrix of shape `(out, in)` is a linear map from `in`-dimensional to
`out`-dimensional space: it can rotate, stretch, shear, project and change dimension, but it keeps the
origin fixed and keeps straight lines straight. Its columns say where each input direction is sent;
each output is a weighted sum of the inputs. The bias then shifts the result (an affine map). Without
activations, a stack of layers collapses by associativity into one matrix $\mathbf{W}_L \cdots \mathbf{W}_1$,
so depth adds no expressive power: the network could only ever draw flat decision boundaries.
Non-linearities like ReLU bend the space between maps, which is what lets compositions represent curved
functions.
**Weak answer:** "The weights learn the important features, and activations make the network deep."
**Why the weak one fails:** it gives no geometric meaning and does not identify the collapse of
linear layers, which is the actual reason activations are needed.
**Follow-up an interviewer would ask:** "If the layer's output dimension is smaller than its input,
what information is lost, and can a later layer recover it?" (No: a map to fewer dimensions has a null
space; distinct inputs that differ by a null-space vector become identical.)

### 2. How do you bracket $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{v}$ for $\mathbf{A}$ `(1000, 10)`, $\mathbf{B}$ `(10, 1000)`, $\mathbf{v}$ `(1000,)`?

**Strong answer:** As $\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{B}\mathbf{v})$. $\mathbf{B}\mathbf{v}$ costs $10 \times 1000 = 10^4$
multiply-adds and gives a `(10,)` vector; $\mathbf{A}$ times that costs another $10^4$, so $2 \times 10^4$
in total with tiny intermediates. $(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B})\mathbf{v}$ first builds a `(1000, 1000)` matrix:
$1000 \times 10 \times 1000 = 10^7$ multiply-adds and $10^6$ floats (8 MB in float64), then $10^6$ more
for the product with $\mathbf{v}$. Same result by associativity, roughly 500 times the work. This
"low-rank" shape is common (LoRA adapters, factorized embeddings), and the bracketing matters at scale.
**Weak answer:** "Left to right, as written; NumPy optimizes it."
**Why the weak one fails:** `@` evaluates left to right and does not reorder; the candidate cannot
estimate cost with the $n\,d\,k$ rule.
**Follow-up an interviewer would ask:** "When *would* you precompute $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}$?" (When you
apply it to many vectors and $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}$ is cheaper to store and apply than the two factors,
which is not the case here, since the factors hold $2 \times 10^4$ numbers versus $10^6$.)

### 3. Why avoid computing a matrix inverse? What do you do instead?

**Strong answer:** You almost never need the inverse itself, only its action on a vector:
$\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{b}$. Computing $\mathbf{A}^{-1}$ explicitly costs more (about three
times the work of one factorization and solve) and is less accurate: rounding errors are amplified by
the condition number, and the explicit inverse adds more error than solving by elimination with
pivoting. For near-singular matrices the difference becomes large. Use `np.linalg.solve` (LU with
partial pivoting), a Cholesky solve for symmetric positive-definite matrices, or `lstsq` / QR / SVD
for least squares. Factor once and reuse the factorization if you solve many right-hand sides.
**Weak answer:** "Inverses are slow, so we use `pinv`."
**Why the weak one fails:** `pinv` is even more expensive (an SVD), the accuracy argument is missing,
and it does not name the right tool for a square system.
**Follow-up an interviewer would ask:** "Is there a case where you do want the inverse?" (Rarely: for
example when you need its entries themselves, such as the diagonal of $(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1}$
for coefficient standard errors, and even then you get it from a factorization.)

### 4. What is multicollinearity, how does it show up, and what do you do?

**Strong answer:** Multicollinearity means some feature columns are (nearly) linear combinations of
others: duplicated measurements in different units, a total stored with its parts, or one-hot columns
plus an intercept. Exactly dependent columns make $\mathbf{X}$ rank-deficient and $\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}$
singular, so the coefficients are not unique; nearly dependent columns make them unstable: large,
with opposite signs, and changing a lot when you refit on slightly different data, while predictions
stay fine. You detect it with `matrix_rank(X) < d`, a large condition number, high pairwise
correlations or variance inflation factors. Fixes: drop or combine redundant features, use reference
coding for categories, or regularize (ridge), and do not interpret individual coefficients of
correlated features.
**Weak answer:** "It is when features are correlated with the target; it makes the model overfit."
**Why the weak one fails:** it confuses correlation among features with correlation with the target,
and misstates the symptom: multicollinearity hurts coefficient stability and interpretation more than
prediction.
**Follow-up an interviewer would ask:** "Your model's predictions are good. Do you still care?" (Only if
you interpret or act on coefficients, or if the correlation structure may change in production.)

### 5. What does singular mean, and how do you detect "almost singular" in practice?

**Strong answer:** A square matrix is singular when it has no inverse: its columns are linearly
dependent, its rank is below its size, its determinant is zero, and the map squashes space onto a
lower-dimensional subspace, so some non-zero vector maps to zero and $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$
has either no solution or infinitely many. In floating point, exact singularity is rare; the practical
problem is near-singularity. Do not use the determinant to detect it (it scales with the matrix size
and units); use the condition number (`np.linalg.cond`) or the smallest singular values, and
`matrix_rank` with a tolerance. A condition number near $10^{k}$ means you can lose about $k$ of the
roughly 16 significant digits of float64.
**Weak answer:** "It is singular if the determinant is 0, so I check `np.linalg.det(A) == 0`."
**Why the weak one fails:** exact float comparison almost never fires, and a tiny determinant does not
mean near-singular (scale a well-conditioned 100 × 100 matrix by 0.1 and its determinant is $10^{-100}$).
**Follow-up an interviewer would ask:** "What do you do when the matrix in your solver is
ill-conditioned?" (Remove redundant features, rescale, regularize with $\lambda\mathbf{I}$, or switch to
an SVD-based least-squares solve.)

### 6. Where do the normal equations come from, and when would you not solve them directly?

**Strong answer:** We want $\mathbf{w}$ minimizing $\lVert\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\mathbf{w}\rVert^2$. The
vector $\mathbf{X}\mathbf{w}$ ranges over the column space of $\mathbf{X}$, and the closest point to
$\mathbf{y}$ is the one whose residual is orthogonal to every column:
$\mathbf{X}^\top(\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\mathbf{w}) = \mathbf{0}$, i.e. $\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}\mathbf{w} = \mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{y}$.
Setting the gradient of the loss to zero gives the same equations. I would not form
$\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}$ when features are nearly collinear, because that squares the condition
number; use QR or SVD (`lstsq`) instead. I would not use a closed form at all when $d$ is very large
(forming and factoring $d \times d$ is $O(nd^2 + d^3)$), when data does not fit in memory, or for models
without a closed form; then use (stochastic) gradient descent.
**Weak answer:** "You take the derivative and set it to zero; it always works if you use the inverse."
**Why the weak one fails:** no geometry or derivation, and it recommends `inv` while missing every
numerical and scale limitation.
**Follow-up an interviewer would ask:** "What changes in the equations with L2 regularization?"
($\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X} + \lambda\mathbf{I}$, which is always invertible for $\lambda > 0$.)

### 7. The residual plot shows a funnel. What does it mean and what do you do?

**Strong answer:** A funnel (spread growing with the fitted value) means the noise is not constant:
heteroscedasticity. Least squares still gives an unbiased line if the model is otherwise right, but it
gives every point equal weight even though high-value points are much noisier, so the estimates are
less efficient and the usual standard errors and prediction intervals are wrong: intervals are too
wide at the low end and too narrow at the high end. Typical causes: a multiplicative process (errors
proportional to size, as with prices or counts). Fixes: model the log of the target (turning
multiplicative noise into additive), use weighted least squares with weights inversely proportional to
the variance, or use robust standard errors; and evaluate errors in relative terms if that matches the
business cost.
**Weak answer:** "There is an outlier; remove the points with large residuals."
**Why the weak one fails:** a funnel is a systematic pattern, not individual outliers; deleting the
large residuals biases the model and hides the problem.
**Follow-up an interviewer would ask:** "After a log transform, how do you turn predictions back into
the original units?" (Exponentiate, and know that $e^{\mathbb{E}[\log y]}$ estimates the median, not the
mean; a correction is needed for the mean.)

### 8. More features than rows ($d > n$): what happens with least squares, and how do you handle it?

**Strong answer:** The `(n, d)` matrix $\mathbf{X}$ has rank at most $n < d$, so $\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}$
`(d, d)` is singular and the system $\mathbf{X}\mathbf{w} = \mathbf{y}$ typically has infinitely many
*exact* solutions: zero training error, a null space of dimension at least $d - n$, and coefficients that
are not determined by the data. The fit memorizes noise and generalizes badly. `lstsq` returns the
minimum-norm solution, which is one implicit choice. Handle it with regularization (ridge adds
$\lambda\mathbf{I}$ and makes the solution unique; lasso also selects features), dimensionality
reduction (PCA), feature selection, or collecting more data, and evaluate on held-out data rather than
training error.
**Weak answer:** "The model has more information, so it will be more accurate."
**Why the weak one fails:** it misses the rank argument and the overfitting it implies; a perfect
training fit is the symptom, not success.
**Follow-up an interviewer would ask:** "Which solution does `np.linalg.lstsq` pick, and why might that
be reasonable?" (The one with the smallest $\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert$, the limit of ridge as $\lambda \to 0$.)
