## Part 2: Interview questions

### 1. Explain precision, recall, ROC-AUC and PR-AUC. Which would you report for a fraud model, and why?

**Strong answer:** At a threshold, precision is the share of flagged cases that are really fraud, TP/(TP + FP), and recall is the share of frauds caught, TP/P. Moving the threshold trades one for
the other. ROC-AUC summarizes all thresholds as the probability that a random fraud scores above a random legitimate transaction; it uses FPR, which divides by the huge number of legitimate
transactions. PR-AUC (average precision) averages precision over the recall levels, and its baseline is the fraud rate, not 0.5. For fraud, with prevalence often 0.1% or below, I would report average precision and,
more usefully, precision and recall at the operating point: the threshold that fits the review team's capacity or the cost ratio. Each comes with a bootstrap interval, because there are few positives.
ROC-AUC can be 0.98 while precision is under 10%.
**Weak answer:** "Precision is how accurate the positives are and recall is how many you find. ROC-AUC is the standard metric, so I'd report that; higher is better."
**Why the weak one fails:** it does not connect the metrics to prevalence or to an operating point, and it picks the metric that hides the problem in fraud.
**Follow-up an interviewer would ask:** "The review team can handle 500 cases a day. How does that change what you report?"

### 2. What does it mean for a classifier to be calibrated? How do you check it, fix it, and when does it matter?

**Strong answer:** Calibrated means the predicted probabilities match observed frequencies: among cases scored around 0.3, about 30% are positive. Check it with a reliability diagram (bin the predictions, plot the mean predicted
probability against the observed rate per bin, using equal-count bins when positives are rare) and with a proper score such as the Brier score or log-loss, compared with the prevalence
baseline $\pi(1 - \pi)$. Fix it with Platt scaling (a logistic fit on the score) or isotonic regression, fitted on held-out data or by cross-fitting (`CalibratedClassifierCV`); both are monotone, so the ranking barely changes.
It matters whenever the number itself is used: expected-loss calculations, pricing, risk tiers, thresholds from a cost formula, or showing a risk to a person. Naive Bayes, SVMs and models trained with class weights or resampling are usually
miscalibrated. If you only rank and take the top $k$, calibration does not change who is picked.
**Weak answer:** "A calibrated model has a good accuracy. You can calibrate it by tuning the threshold."
**Why the weak one fails:** it confuses calibration with accuracy and with thresholding, and gives no way to measure it.
**Follow-up an interviewer would ask:** "Why can't ROC-AUC detect miscalibration?"

### 3. How do you choose the decision threshold for a classifier going into production?

**Strong answer:** From the decision, not from 0.5. First get the costs (or values) of a false positive and a false negative from the business, or a capacity constraint such as reviews per day. With calibrated
probabilities, the expected-cost-minimizing threshold is $c_{FP}/(c_{FP} + c_{FN})$. In practice I compute the average cost (or precision and recall) for every threshold on out-of-fold or validation predictions and pick the minimum, or
the threshold that fills capacity. Then I confirm it once on the test set and report the confusion matrix there. I check how flat the cost curve is near the optimum, because a flat curve means the exact value
matters little. In production I monitor the flag rate and precision, since drift moves the best threshold, and I re-tune when the costs or the base rate change. If there are groups to protect, I check the error rates
per group at that threshold (B4.6).
**Weak answer:** "Use 0.5, or pick the threshold that maximizes F1 on the test set."
**Why the weak one fails:** 0.5 ignores costs and prevalence, F1 assumes a cost trade-off nobody stated, and tuning on the test set biases the reported result.
**Follow-up an interviewer would ask:** "Your model was trained with class weights. Does the formula still apply?"

### 4. Churn from monthly snapshots (one row per customer per month): how do you set up validation?

**Strong answer:** Two kinds of dependence matter: the same customer appears in many rows, and the data is ordered in time. The model will predict next month's churn for current customers using the past.
So I hold out the most recent months as the test set, and train only on months before it, with a gap equal to the label window (if churn means "leaves within 30 days", training labels must be known before the test period starts).
For development, I use time-ordered folds (an expanding window); within them, I check whether the same customers appearing in train and validation inflates scores. If the model will also score new customers, I group by customer. Features must be
computed as of each snapshot date (no future aggregates), inside the pipeline. I stratify or at least monitor the churn rate per fold, use one fixed search budget for every model, and evaluate the final model once on
the held-out months with a bootstrap interval over customers.
**Weak answer:** "Use stratified 5-fold cross-validation on all the rows, since churn is imbalanced."
**Why the weak one fails:** stratification handles imbalance but ignores both leaks: customers on both sides of the split and future months in training.
**Follow-up an interviewer would ask:** "How would you detect that a feature is computed with future information?"

### 5. Why is the best CV score from a search optimistic, what do you do about it, and when would you use Bayesian search?

**Strong answer:** Each configuration's CV score is its true score plus noise from the particular folds. Picking the best of many picks the luckiest, so its score is biased upward. With $B$ similar configurations and noise $\sigma$, the bias is
roughly $c_B\sigma$ ($c_{20} \approx 1.87$), and it grows with the number of configurations and the noise. Remedies: keep an untouched test set and evaluate once at the end; use nested cross-validation (tune inside each outer fold, score on the outer fold) when you need an estimate of
the whole tuning procedure; fix the search budget in advance; and prefer simpler settings within one standard deviation of the best. Grid search wastes evaluations when only a few settings matter; random search covers each setting better with the same budget;
Bayesian search such as Optuna's TPE models which regions gave good results and proposes new points there, so it usually finds good settings in fewer trials. That is worth it when each evaluation is expensive.
**Weak answer:** "The CV score is an average over folds, so it's reliable. I'd use grid search because it's exhaustive."
**Why the weak one fails:** it misses selection bias entirely, and "exhaustive" grids are exhaustive only over the few values chosen.
**Follow-up an interviewer would ask:** "Nested CV gave you a number. Which model do you ship?"

### 6. How do you handle a dataset where 1% of the labels are positive?

**Strong answer:** Start with evaluation, not resampling. Use stratified splits; use metrics that see the rare class, such as average precision, recall at a target precision, or expected cost, with
bootstrap intervals because positives are few; and compare against the trivial baselines. Train a well-calibrated model without rebalancing first, then choose the threshold from costs or capacity on out-of-fold predictions; that alone usually fixes "the model never
predicts positive". Class weights or over- and undersampling multiply the model's odds by the weight: they are an implicit threshold and cost ratio, and they break calibration, so I would use them only if they improve the metric on
validation (they can help a model that underfits the minority class), and then recalibrate or apply the prior correction. Any resampling happens inside the training folds only. If positives are too rare to learn from, consider
anomaly detection or collecting more labelled positives.
**Weak answer:** "Use SMOTE to balance the classes, then train normally and use accuracy."
**Why the weak one fails:** it starts with resampling, ignores the metric and the threshold, and keeps accuracy, which is meaningless at 1%.
**Follow-up an interviewer would ask:** "After class weighting, a stakeholder asks for the predicted probability of each case. What do you give them?"

### 7. 99% training accuracy and 80% validation accuracy: what would you do?

**Strong answer:** A 19-point gap points to high variance (overfitting), but first I would check the setup. What is the base rate? If 80% of the labels are one class, 80% validation accuracy is no better than the baseline. Is
validation split correctly (grouped, time-ordered)? Is there leakage that inflates training performance? Then I would draw a cross-validated learning curve. If the validation error is still falling with more data, more data helps. And I would draw a validation curve for the
capacity setting (depth, number of trees, regularization strength) to find where validation error bottoms out. Remedies in order of cost: regularize or simplify (shallower trees, larger `min_samples_leaf`, a stronger
penalty, early stopping), average models (bagging, random forests), add data. I would not add capacity or many new features until the gap closes. And I would switch to log-loss or AP for reading the curves if classes are imbalanced.
**Weak answer:** "The model needs to be bigger, or train it longer, to get validation accuracy up too."
**Why the weak one fails:** it prescribes the opposite of what a variance problem needs, and it checks neither the metric nor the split.
**Follow-up an interviewer would ask:** "The learning curve shows the validation error flat from 20% of the data onwards. What now?"

### 8. How would you audit a model for fairness before deployment, and how would you pick the metric?

**Strong answer:** First define the decision, the affected people, the sensitive attributes (collected lawfully, used for auditing even if not as features) and which error harms whom. Then, at the chosen threshold, compute per group:
group size, base rate, selection rate, TPR and FPR, with bootstrap intervals for small groups, and the gaps between groups (demographic parity, equal opportunity and equalized odds differences, plus the selection-rate ratio).
I would check proxies by trying to predict the attribute from the other features, and remember that dropping the attribute does not remove gaps. When base rates differ, the definitions conflict:
equal selection rates and equal error rates cannot both hold, so the choice is a policy one made with stakeholders and legal review. For a benefit such as a loan or an interview, equal opportunity (equal TPR among the qualified) is often
prioritized; when false positives harm people (fraud flags), FPR parity. Mitigations include per-group thresholds, reweighting or constrained training; each moves the other metrics, so report all of them. Everything goes into the model card.
**Weak answer:** "Remove sex and race from the features; then the model is fair. Check that overall accuracy is high."
**Why the weak one fails:** fairness through unawareness ignores proxies, overall accuracy hides group gaps, and no metric or trade-off is considered.
**Follow-up an interviewer would ask:** "A group with 40 people shows a TPR gap of 0.15. What do you report?"
