## Part 1: Self-test

1. **The SVD gives the best rank-$k$ fit of a *complete* matrix, so with zeros in the gaps it spends its capacity reproducing 1.5 million invented
   zeros and predicts values near 0 for most unseen pairs. MF's objective sums only over the observed ratings (plus an L2 penalty), so empty cells
   contribute nothing.** In the lab: SVD with zeros RMSE 2.54 (worse than the global mean, 1.21); SVD after filling with the bias model 1.026; MF 1.002.
   *Revisit: B6.1, A3.2.*
2. **Explicit feedback is a stated preference (stars); implicit feedback is behaviour (views, clicks, purchases), which shows interest but whose absence
   does not show dislike. With only positives a model can score everything as 1, so negative sampling draws a few random unseen items per positive as
   $y = 0$, fresh each epoch: mostly right, and no item is consistently called disliked.** The RMSE-best explicit MF ranked films by predicted *rating*,
   so it recommended highly rated films few people watch (*Pather Panchali*, 8 viewers): recall@10 0.019 against 0.067 for popularity and 0.120 for
   implicit MF. *Revisit: B6.1.*
3. **Retrieval narrows a huge catalogue to $N$ candidates cheaply (dot products, an ANN index) and must have high recall@$N$; ranking reorders those $N$
   with a feature-rich model and must have high precision at the top (NDCG@k, MAP@k, MRR).** An item the retriever did not return is not among the
   candidates, so no reordering can put it in the list: the reranker's recall@k ≤ the retriever's recall@$N$ for $k \le N$ (lab: recall@100 0.55 on the
   test block). *Revisit: B6.2.*
4. **At forecast time the newest value you know is 12 months older than the target, so a younger feature uses data the model will not have in
   production: offline scores look good and production ones do not.** Two pandas traps: an off-by-one order (`s.rolling(12).mean().shift(11)` includes
   $y_{t-11}$) and a centred window (`rolling(..., center=True)`); also filling gaps with interpolation or a whole-column mean. The test: compute the
   features, overwrite every value newer than $t - h$ with garbage, recompute, and assert that row $t$ is unchanged, for many $t$. *Revisit: B6.3, B1.4.*
5. **A tree predicts the mean of the training targets in a leaf, and boosting adds up leaf values fitted to training targets and residuals, so its output

## Part 2: Interview questions

### 1. How would you build the first version of a product recommender for an e-commerce site, and how would you know it works?

**Strong answer:** Start from the data and the surface: what slot is being filled (home page, "similar items", email), and which logged signals
exist (views, add-to-cart, purchases), weighted by how strongly they show intent. First version: a popularity baseline (per category, recent window) and
an item-to-item model ("people who bought X also bought Y" from co-occurrence or item-based CF), because it is simple, explainable and covers anonymous
users. Then implicit matrix factorization (sampled negatives or weighted ALS) for logged-in users, whose factors also serve as retrieval embeddings. Evaluate
offline on a time split (train up to a date, test on the next weeks) with recall@k and NDCG@k against the popularity baseline, plus coverage; then an A/B
test on the business metric (click-through, conversion, revenue per session), because offline metrics only score items users happened to see.
**Weak answer:** "Use collaborative filtering with matrix factorization and measure RMSE."
**Why the weak one fails:** no baseline, no thought about implicit signals or the product surface, RMSE instead of a ranking metric, and no time split or
online test.
**Follow-up an interviewer would ask:** "Your offline NDCG improved 10% but the A/B test is flat. Why might that be?"

### 2. How do you handle cold start for new users and for new items?

**Strong answer:** New users have no history: show popular or trending items (by region or traffic source), ask for a few preferences at onboarding, and
use context (device, referrer, the first clicks in the session) to personalize quickly; switch to the personal model once there are enough interactions.
New items have no interactions, so pure collaborative filtering cannot score them: use content features (category, text and image embeddings, price) in a
hybrid model that maps item attributes into the same space as the factors, and give new items some guaranteed exposure (an exploration slot or a boost) so
they collect data. Monitor the share of traffic and catalogue that is cold, and evaluate those segments separately.
**Weak answer:** "Recommend the most popular items until there is enough data."
**Why the weak one fails:** handles only new users, and popularity alone keeps new items invisible forever (a feedback loop).
**Follow-up an interviewer would ask:** "How would you decide how much traffic to spend on exploring new items?"

### 3. Explain NDCG. When would you report it instead of precision@k or MRR, and what is the most common way to compute it wrongly?

**Strong answer:** NDCG@k sums the relevance of the items in the top $k$, each divided by $\log_2(\text{position} + 1)$ so that lower positions count less
(DCG), and divides by the DCG of the ideal ordering of the user's relevant items, so a perfect list scores 1. Unlike precision@k it rewards putting the best
items first, and unlike precision, recall or MRR it handles graded relevance (perfect, partial, marginal). I use precision@k when exactly $k$ slots are shown
and only the count matters, MRR when there is one right answer (question answering), and NDCG for ranked lists with several relevant items or grades. The
common bug: building the ideal list only from the candidates the system retrieved, which ignores relevant items the retriever missed and inflates the score
(42% in the B6.2 lab); also off-by-one discounts ($\log_2 j$ instead of $\log_2(j + 1)$) and not stating linear versus exponential gain.
**Weak answer:** "NDCG measures ranking quality between 0 and 1; higher is better."
**Why the weak one fails:** no definition of the discount or normalization, no comparison with other metrics, no pitfalls.
**Follow-up an interviewer would ask:** "Your NDCG@10 is 0.14. Is that good?"

### 4. Design the ranking system behind a search box over 10 million products. What happens in each stage, and how do you evaluate each one?

**Strong answer:** Two or three stages. Retrieval: lexical search (BM25) plus embedding search (query and product embeddings in an approximate
nearest-neighbour index) returns a few hundred to a thousand candidates in milliseconds; evaluated by recall@N against judged relevant products and
latency. Ranking: a learning-to-rank model (LightGBM LambdaRank, or a neural cross-encoder for the top few dozen) on features of the query, the product
and the pair (text match, price, popularity, the user's history, freshness, availability); evaluated by NDCG@10 and MRR on held-out queries, with labels
from clicks and purchases corrected for position bias (users click the top more because it is the top) or from human relevance judgments. Business rules
(stock, compliance) are applied last. Finally an A/B test on conversion and zero-result rate, monitoring latency at the 95th percentile.
**Weak answer:** "Embed everything with a transformer and return the nearest products."
**Why the weak one fails:** a single stage with no ranking features, no evaluation per stage, no mention of labels, position bias or latency.
**Follow-up an interviewer would ask:** "How do you get relevance labels without paying people to judge millions of queries?"

### 5. How do you validate a forecasting model? Why is ordinary k-fold cross-validation wrong here, and what do you report?

**Strong answer:** Walk-forward (rolling-origin) validation: choose several origins in the recent past, train only on data up to each origin, forecast the
real horizon, score, move forward; refit at every origin as production would. K-fold shuffles time: each test row has training rows from its future, so
the model interpolates instead of forecasting and the score is optimistic (a third too low in the B6.3 lab); it also hides feature leakage. I report the
error per fold and per horizon step, the mean, a scale-free comparison with seasonal naive (skill score or MASE), and I check that every feature is at least
as old as the horizon with an automated test.
**Weak answer:** "Use an 80/20 split and report RMSE on the test set."
**Why the weak one fails:** if random, it leaks the future; if a single time split, it gives one noisy number; no baseline, no horizon.
**Follow-up an interviewer would ask:** "When would you use a sliding window instead of an expanding one?"

### 6. A demand-forecasting model had a 5% error offline and 20% in its first month in production. How do you debug it?

**Strong answer:** First suspect the evaluation, then the data, then the world. Evaluation: was the offline test a random split, or a time split with a
horizon shorter than production's? Re-run walk-forward with the real horizon. Features: does any feature use information not available at forecast time
(a lag younger than the horizon, observed rather than forecast weather, promotions entered after the fact, a scaler or target encoding fitted on all data)?
Run the scramble test and compare the feature values computed offline with those logged in production for the same dates (train-serve skew). Data: late
or missing data at forecast time, changed units or definitions upstream. World: a structural break (new pricing, a competitor, a stock-out that censored
demand). Compare against seasonal naive over the same month: if the baseline also degraded, it is the world; if only the model did, it is the model or its features.
**Weak answer:** "The model is overfitting; add regularization and retrain on more data."
**Why the weak one fails:** jumps to a fix without diagnosis; a 4× gap between offline and production almost always comes from leakage or skew, which
regularization does not fix.
**Follow-up an interviewer would ask:** "How would you stop this from happening again?"

### 7. ARIMA or gradient boosting for a forecasting problem: how do you decide?

**Strong answer:** It depends on the number of series, the history length and the drivers. Classical models (ETS, seasonal ARIMA) fit one series with a few
parameters, extrapolate trend naturally and do well on short, smooth single series; in the B6.4 lab Holt–Winters beat LightGBM on CO2, and its lead held with
5–9 years of history. Gradient boosting on lag features is a global model: it learns across many related series and uses known drivers with non-linear
effects (promotions, prices, weather forecasts, holidays); in the lab it won clearly on a demand series with such drivers. Trees cannot extrapolate, so
model differences on trending series. In practice I compute seasonal naive, fit both families under the same walk-forward folds with the same information,
and choose by error, operational cost and interpretability; often the answer is a global model with a classical fallback for series with little data.
**Weak answer:** "XGBoost, because it wins Kaggle competitions."
**Why the weak one fails:** no conditions, ignores short histories and trend extrapolation, and no validation plan.
**Follow-up an interviewer would ask:** "You have 50,000 SKUs, many with fewer than 20 weeks of history. What do you do?"

### 8. `[RAI]` A recommender keeps showing the same popular items, and new sellers complain they get no exposure. What is happening, how do you measure it, and what would you change?

**Strong answer:** A feedback loop with popularity bias: the model is trained on interactions with what it chose to show, popular items get shown and
clicked more, which makes them look even better, and new items never collect the interactions needed to be recommended. Measure catalogue coverage (share of
items shown at all), the concentration of exposure (share of impressions going to the top 1% of items, or a Gini coefficient), and exposure and conversion by
seller group (new versus established, small versus large), over time. Changes: reserve a share of slots for exploration (for example a bandit or randomized
slots, with the data logged so it can be used for unbiased evaluation), add content features so new items can be scored, correct training for exposure
(weight interactions by the inverse of their probability of being shown), and add diversity or fairness-of-exposure constraints to the final ranking. Run an
A/B test, since these changes trade some short-term clicks for long-term marketplace health, and agree the target with the business.
**Weak answer:** "Add some randomness to the recommendations."
**Why the weak one fails:** names a partial fix without explaining the loop, without metrics and without saying how to know whether it helped.
**Follow-up an interviewer would ask:** "How would you evaluate a new model offline when your logs only contain what the old model chose to show?"
